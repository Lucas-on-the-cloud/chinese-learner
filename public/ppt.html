The Scenario: A Lost Hiker

The bottleneck is detection, not flight

A hiker doesn't return by sunset. Search radius grows quadratically with elapsed time — within hours, dozens of km² must be covered.

Ground teams move at 3–4 km/h. A drone covers 30 km/h — an order of magnitude faster.

But a human operator cannot reliably spot a 30-pixel person in hours of live drone footage. Automated detection is the real bottleneck.

Why It's Hard — Three Constraints (press space to reveal each)

1

Tiny objects

Person ≈ 30 px in a 4000×3000 image.

That is < 0.1% of image area.

On a phone screen, ≈ the size of a fingertip nail.

2

Natural decoys

Rocks, huts, vegetation share person-like silhouettes from above.

False positives in SAR mean wasted resources sent to wrong locations.

3

Scarce data

HERIDAL: 1,124 train images.

COCO: 118,000 train images.

100× smaller — data-hungry methods are off the table.

These three constraints drive every design choice in the project.
Presenter A · Setup04 / 16

Our Approach: Pipeline, Not New Architecture

Path A — Design a new detector

  • New architecture, attention modules
  • New loss functions, custom backbone
  • The most common path in aerial small-object literature
  • Risk: novel architectures overfit in 1,124-image regime

Path B — Improve AROUND a fixed detector ⭐

  • YOLOv12-s, released weights, never modified
  • Change training data, preprocessing, inference, post-processing
  • Every Δ attributable to one component
  • Pipeline portable to any detector (YOLOv11, RT-DETR, etc.)
We chose Path B. The detector is held constant across every phase below — all improvements come from outside the model.

The localization gap

mAP@0.5 = 0.759 means we find persons.

mAP@0.5:0.95 = 0.344 means boxes are not tight enough.

Why? 4000 → 640 resize shrinks a 30-px person to ~5 px. Detector never sees real-scale persons.

Diagnosis: the model has a fundamental scale mismatch between training (5-px persons) and the real deployment distribution (30-px persons).
Presenter B · Method08 / 16

The Hidden Problem: Train–Inference Scale Mismatch

SAHI alone is not enough. The model must also be TRAINED at native resolution — otherwise inference is out-of-distribution.
Presenter B · Method09 / 16

Plan A — Couple Training and Inference Scales ⭐

01

Native-resolution crops

From each 4000×3000 image: 2 person-centered + 1 random crops of 640×640.

NO resize. ~4,500 crops from 1,124 images.

→
02

Train YOLOv12-s on crops

Same hyperparameters as baseline.

80 epochs · AdamW · lr 10⁻³ · batch 16 · AMP · ~3h on T4

→
03

SAHI inference on full image

Original 4000×3000 val → 640×640 tiles, 20% overlap.

~48 tiles per image · NMS merge with τ = 0.5

Training distribution = Inference distribution. Both are native-resolution 640×640 patches; persons appear at ~30 px in both. The two halves are now coupled by construction.

Presenter C · Lesson14 / 16

Three Failures, One Convergent Lesson

Three different post-hoc additions on top of Plan A failed for three different mechanical reasons.

Convergent lesson: Plan A's scale alignment is the load-bearing piece of the pipeline. Any post-hoc addition that doesn't preserve scale-awareness breaks something — each for its own specific reason.
Presenter C · Closing15 / 16

Three Takeaways

1

Train–inference distribution alignment is the dominant lever

In aerial small-object detection, matching the data scale at training and inference matters more than architectural change. Plan A's +0.113 mAP@0.5 comes from this alone, with no new model.

2

SAHI works only when paired with native-resolution training

Naive SAHI alone: 0.759 → 0.255. The technique cannot be treated as an inference-only drop-in. The two halves are coupled, not independent.

3

Foundation models don't transfer for free in this domain

Both SDXL (Phase 3) and SAM2 (Phase 4) required scale-aware adaptation that we did not provide. Drop-in use of generic foundation models is not sufficient for the small-aerial-person setting.
